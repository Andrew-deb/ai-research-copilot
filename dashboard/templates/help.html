Do I need an account to try it?

No. You can browse the corpus, open any paper, run semantic search and ask a limited number of questions without signing in. An account is what lets you keep things: collections, notes and reading progress.

Keyword search or semantic search?

Keyword search matches the words you typed. Semantic search matches meaning, so a question about models inventing facts finds papers on hallucination and grounding even when none of those words appear in your query. Semantic is the better default for a question; keyword is better when you already know the title.

What does the percentage next to a result mean?

How closely your question matches the best passage in that paper. Higher is closer. A badge reading from the conclusion means the match came from that section rather than the abstract — usually a sign the paper addresses your question rather than merely mentioning the topic.

Why do some papers have no sections?

Because no readable open-access copy exists: there is no free copy, the publisher declines automated access, or the PDF is a scan with no text layer. Those papers stay searchable by abstract — nothing is lost, there is simply less to search.

Can I trust the answers?

Every answer cites the papers it drew from, and you can open each one to check. The assistant answers from the indexed corpus only; when the corpus cannot answer a question it says so rather than filling the gap.

What happens when I hit a daily limit?

Search and answers pause until the next day; browsing, collections and notes keep working. Logging in raises the allowance — see plans.

How do I build a reading list?

Add papers to a collection from any paper page, then open the collection and choose Generate reading plan. It sequences them oldest first, weighted by citation impact, so foundations come before the variants that build on them. You can drag to reorder afterwards.

Something looks wrong or missing

The corpus is deliberately narrow and still growing, so a missing paper is more likely to be out of scope than a bug. If an answer looks wrong, open its citations — that usually shows what the assistant actually read.
